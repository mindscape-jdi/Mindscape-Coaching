Level ground: communication is only possible between equals

A teeter-totter is a lever on a fulcrum. Two people can meet eye to eye on it only when their weights balance. A coaching session works the same way: I sit on one side with my attention, my experience and my time, and the person across from me balances that weight by paying a share of their own means. Only then can either of us speak without looking up or down.

A share, not a price

A flat price weighs differently on every person who pays it. The same $100 can keep someone out of coaching entirely and register as nothing to someone else. The formula instead takes the same share of everyone’s yearly means, so the weight each client puts on the plank feels the same to them: one eight-hundredth of a year for a session, one eightieth for a season.

Yearly means count income plus 4% of net worth. Four percent follows the planner William Bengen’s “safe withdrawal rate” (1994): roughly what savings can give up each year without shrinking over a lifetime. Counting it treats wealth as a second income, so a client living on assets rather than wages still sits at their true weight.

Finland sets traffic fines the same way. Its day-fines scale with income, and in 2002 a Nokia executive, Anssi Vanjoki, was fined €116,000 for riding a motorcycle 75 km/h in a 50 zone. The aim was not a bigger penalty but an equal one, felt the same by whoever paid it.

Both ends of the plank

The plank tips in two directions, and either one ends the conversation. Charge too much, and a client sits low, looking up, grateful or resentful, but not free to speak plainly. Charge too little, and they sit high, receiving a favour they cannot return. The anthropologist Marcel Mauss showed in The Gift (1925) that an unreturned gift creates a debt and a rank: the giver rises and the receiver falls.

The high end matters just as much as the low. A client with $100 million or $1 billion who pays a flat fee pays almost nothing for what they receive, and both of us feel the tilt: they become a patron to be pleased or a customer to be served, and a coach who must please cannot tell the truth. A fee scaled to their means lets them sit level and be met as a person rather than a purse.

Who the fee funds

The fees at the high end fund this work for the whole community. When a billionaire’s season comes to $500,000, that sum does not raise them above the room; it holds up the rest of the plank. It makes room for clients who arrive with little, who still pay their own share of their own means and so sit level too.

Paid this way, the money carries no favour and buys no rank. It lets wealthy clients give fully without becoming owners of the coach, or of the people their fees reach.
